If you only have a few days

Short Lycian Way sections worth choosing

Most walkers never do the full trail. These are the stretches worth choosing if you have two days or a week

Ovacık to Faralya ★★★★★1 day · moderateOne of the best single days
Planning stage 01

The most popular day walk on the trail and the easiest to reach from Fethiye. Climbs out of Ölüdeniz onto the ridge above Butterfly Valley with the lagoon behind you. Faralya sits on a road, so getting back is straightforward, though check local minibus times on the day rather than trusting a timetable. If you want one day on the Lycian Way, this is the usual choice.

Faralya to Kabak ★★★★★1 day · easy to moderateEasy first taste
Part of planning stage 02 · Faralya to Alınca

Short, scenic and manageable for most fitness levels, linking two villages that both have accommodation, though we have not verified individual places. Kabak's valley and beach make a natural overnight, and there is a longer coastal variant if you want more time on your feet.

Kabak to Alınca ★★★★☆1 day · demandingThe big view
Part of planning stage 02 · Faralya to Alınca

A hard, spectacular day climbing away from Kabak to the tiny settlement at Alınca, high above the sea with one of the best views on the entire route. Steep and rocky, with limited water. The reward is the sense of leaving the resort coast behind entirely.

Kaş to Üçağız and Kaleköy ★★★★★2 to 3 days · moderateA standout coastal walk
Planning stages 09 to 11 · Kaş to Üçağız

The most rewarding multi-day stretch for scenery. Follows the coast past sunken ruins and empty coves into the fishing villages of Üçağız and Kaleköy, with the castle at Simena above. Rocky underfoot and slower than it looks. Üçağız and Kaleköy are established stops with rooms and boat access, but book ahead in season rather than assuming.

The Patara and Xanthos cluster ★★★★☆1 to 2 days · easyFor history
Planning stages 05 to 06

Not the best walking, but the densest archaeology on the trail: three major ancient cities plus the Delikkemer aqueduct within a short radius, and Patara's sand beach to finish. Best treated as flat, easy days or even as day trips from a base in Kalkan.

Adrasan to Çıralı via Olympos ★★★★★1 day · demandingRuins and flames
Planning stages 16 to 17

A mountain crossing between two holiday villages that finishes by walking straight through the ruins of Olympos, scattered through the trees along a stream. Combine it with the Chimaera flames above Çıralı after dark for the best evening on the trail.

Çıralı to Beycik under Tahtalı ★★★★☆2 days · demandingHigh mountain
Inland variant · not one of the 23 planning stages

Climbs inland beneath Tahtalı Dağı, the ancient Mount Olympos, on the tougher of the two waymarked lines. There is an optional summit push, or you can take the cable car from the coast for the view without the climb. Check accommodation before you start.

Göynük Canyon to Geyikbayırı ★★★★☆2 days · demandingThe finish
Planning stages 22 to 23 · Göynük Canyon to Geyikbayırı

The finish, and a genuine highlight: a rugged descent through Göynük Canyon before the last mountain miles to the end of this guide's route. Water levels can force wading in early spring, and waymarking is weaker here than in the west. Transport to Antalya from Göynük or Geyikbayırı.

Common questions

Lycian Way FAQ: distance, difficulty, water and accommodation

How long is the Lycian Way?

+

The current wider Lycian Way network is about 760 km, as described by the route maintainers, including newer and alternative routes. This guide follows a selected 23-section itinerary from Ovacık to Geyikbayırı of about 447 km, which is stages 2 to 25 of the published stage list. You will also see 540 km quoted: that figure adds the Fethiye approach, the final stage on to Antalya and the lettered alternates. The numbers describe different scopes.

How long does it take to walk the whole trail?

+

Between 25 and 33 days for a full west-to-east walk, depending on pace, rest days and which variants you take. Very few people do the whole thing; the trail was designed to be broken up. The trail is built to be modular, with most stages ending in a village with road access, so the usual approach is to walk one region over three to six days and use public transport to get back.

When is the best time to walk the Lycian Way?

+

February to May and September to November. Current route-maintainer guidance singles out February, which is often warmer and sunnier than March. Summer is too hot for long walks on this terrain. High sections near Tahtalı Dağı and above Demre can hold snow, so leave those until mid-May or later.

Is the Lycian Way difficult?

+

It is not technically difficult and requires no climbing skill, but it is physically demanding. The ground is hard and stony, the gradients are steep, and many stages involve several hundred metres of ascent. Ankle-supporting boots and trekking poles make a real difference on the descents.

Where does the Lycian Way start and end?

+

The official western start is at Ovacık, just above Ölüdeniz and roughly two hours from Dalaman airport. A separate waymarked link runs down from Fethiye through the ghost village of Kayaköy if you want to begin in the town. The 23-section route used in this guide ends at Geyikbayırı. The route maintainers also give Geyikbayırı as the eastern endpoint, and describe daily buses from there toward Antalya; we have not checked current timetables, so confirm locally. Historical and alternative extensions appear in other route descriptions. Older sources sometimes end the route at Hısarçandır instead; we have not been able to verify when or why that changed, so treat the Hısarçandır ending as historical rather than current.

How do you get to the trail, and back again?

+

The western trailhead at Ovacık, just above Ölüdeniz, is about two hours from Dalaman airport. Current maintainer information describes daily buses from Geyikbayırı to Antalya; we have not checked timetables. In between, public transport along the coast is generally described as good, and because nearly every stage finishes in a village with road access, you can walk a section and dolmuş back to your base rather than committing to the full route.

Is there accommodation, or do you have to camp?

+

On most of the trail you can find a bed in village houses, pensions or small hotels, which is unusual for a long-distance route. Do not treat it as guaranteed: several regions have thin or seasonal accommodation, and we have not verified any of it on the ground. Accommodation is very limited or may be absent on the high Demre to Finike crossing. Plan for camping or arranged logistics and verify current options before departure. Inland villages between Çıralı and Göynük are the thinnest for beds, so confirm before you commit to a night there.

Is water available on the trail?

+

Current route-maintainer guidance is that every village has a clean source and that you normally pass at least one public source a day, but that wells and springs start failing from August in dry summers and that the Lycian Way is among the driest routes it maintains. Well water should be purified from June to the end of October. The high Demre to Finike crossing is one of the sections where hikers should plan conservatively for water and accommodation; confirm current sources before departure. Carry extra until you have confirmed the sources ahead are running.

What maps or GPX should you carry?

+

The route is marked with red and white blazes to the standard European convention, but paint fades on exposed sections and reroutes happen, most often where construction or fire has forced a change. Carry a GPS track as insurance. The route maintainers keep the updated route on OpenStreetMap, so an OSM-based app is the most reliable digital option.

What is the best short section if I only have a few days?

+

For one day, Ovacık to Faralya above Butterfly Valley (stage 01). For two or three days, the coastal stretch from Kaş to Üçağız and Kaleköy is the most rewarding walking on the route. For ruins and atmosphere, Adrasan to Çıralı through Olympos, finished with the Chimaera flames after dark.

Do you need a guide?

+

No. The route is waymarked with red and white blazes and most people walk it independently. That said, paint fades on exposed stretches and reroutes happen, so carry a GPS track as backup. First-time long-distance walkers sometimes prefer a guided or self-guided package for the remote sections.

Can you walk the Lycian Way solo?

+

Yes, and many people do. The route passes through villages regularly in most regions and public transport along the coast is good. For a first solo long walk, the busier western regions are the easier introduction; the remote mountain crossing between Demre and Finike is the one stretch to think carefully about going alone.

Is there drinking water on the Lycian Way?

+

Yes, but you have to plan around it. Route-maintainer guidance is that every village has a clean source, either a spring with a drinking trough or the washing facilities at the village mosque, and that on most trails you pass at least one public source a day. Two catches matter. Some sources are wells rather than springs, so a light container and a length of cord earn their weight. And from June to the end of October, while livestock are moved through the hills, well water should be purified with iodine, sterilising tablets or by boiling. In dry summers wells and springs start failing from August, and maintainer guidance describes the Lycian Way as one of the drier long-distance routes in the network.

What boots and clothing do you need for the Lycian Way?

+

Boots are the one item worth spending on. The limestone here is sharp and unforgiving, and route-maintainer guidance is blunt about it: if you are carrying a rucksack, thick-soled boots with ankle support are essential rather than optional. Long trousers and long sleeves also beat shorts on this particular trail, because long stretches pass through thorny maquis that will shred bare legs. In villages, cover shoulders and knees; rural Türkiye is conservative and bare chests in particular are not welcome. From late autumn into early spring, carry waterproofs and a head torch every day.

How many days of food do you need to carry?

+

Two days at most, even on the longest stretch. That is the standing guidance for this route, and it holds because the route threads through settlements rather than wilderness. Where a village has no shop, knocking on a door for bread and cheese is normal and accepted; pay for what you take, and if the man of the house refuses money, give it to the women or a small gift to the children. Do not plan on buying alcohol: few village stores stock beer or rakı and almost none sell wine, and shops anywhere in Türkiye cannot sell alcohol after 10pm.

How reliable is the waymarking, and what if you lose the trail?

+

The red and white blazes are repainted roughly every two years while a route is new and every four to five once it is worn in, so condition varies by section. Marks also disappear for physical reasons: road construction, dam building, landslides and fallen trees. Maintainer guidance notes that waymarking problems are more common after winter weather. If the marks stop, the drill is to retrace to the last one you are sure of and search in a circle of about a hundred metres before assuming the route has changed. Building a small cairn once you find it again is the courtesy that keeps the trail working for the next walker.

Can you take trekking poles, and are they worth it?

+

Worth it for the descents, which are where this trail does knee damage, and for stream crossings and any winter snow on the high sections. The trade-off, which maintainer guidance also notes, is that poles snag in low bushes and scrub, and they are easy to lose.

Can you mountain bike the Lycian Way?

+

No, and the route maintainers say so directly. Nothing in Turkish law stops you trying, but the route is steep, narrow and laid over rough stone and between rocks, and it defeats riders regardless of ability. If you want to ride in this region, the forestry roads through the Beydağları are the realistic option, and there are signposted networks above Kemer and above Konyaaltı with maps produced by those municipalities. Inland waymarked routes such as the Phrygian Way and the Evliya Çelebi Way are the ones actually built with bikes in mind.

Is the Lycian Way suitable for children?

+

Under about twelve, a long-distance trek is usually beyond both the stamina and the patience. Maintainer guidance recommends basing yourself somewhere and walking out from it rather than moving every day, and for this trail it names Çıralı, Kaş or Üçağız, and Kabak as the places to do that. All three give you swimming, boats, ruins and short waymarked walks within reach, which is a better week for a family than a forced march.

Which months should you avoid on the Lycian Way?

+

Mid-December to the end of January, which current route-maintainer guidance advises against outright because storms and snowfall hit most of Türkiye in that window. Summer is a heat problem rather than a weather one. Remember that altitude does much of the work on this route: temperature drops roughly seven degrees for every thousand metres, so a 26 degree day in Antalya is nearer twelve on the pass below Tahtalı. That gap is why the high sections and the coastal ones need planning as separate seasons.

What do you do in an emergency on the trail?

+

Call 112, the single national emergency number, which reaches ambulance, police, fire and rescue. In rural areas the responding force is the Jandarma, on 156. Give coordinates if you have them, which is the practical argument for carrying the route offline. Türkiye also has AKUT, a well-regarded volunteer search and rescue organisation with regional branches, and your consulate can authorise a helicopter search. Use 156 for the Jandarma, or 112 for anything urgent.

Where can you get official GPX files for the Lycian Way?

+

Free GPX is easier to find than it used to be. The trail's own planning platform publishes a per-stage file and a combined full-route file with no signup, and states that these mirror the maintained route data. Community track libraries carry hiker-recorded tracks for individual stages, and at least one independent guide offers a free route file alongside a paid version with water points and camp spots. The maintainers themselves supply GPX or KML to guidebook owners on request and sells printed trekking maps of Lycia. The openly licensed option is the OpenStreetMap route data, which is what the map on this page is drawn from. This page does not offer track downloads; for a file to navigate with, use a current route-maintainer source or another up-to-date navigation source.

Behind the route

How we built this guide

The Lycian Way is described differently depending on where you look. We built this guide by putting those descriptions side by side: current route-maintainer information, specialist trail databases, mapped route geometry, full-route GPS tracks and published stage data, then working out where they agree and where they do not.

Every section was checked for alignment, distance, endpoints, continuity and known variants, and the drawn line was tested against the mapped route rather than assumed to match it. Where the evidence does not line up, we leave the section unresolved rather than smoothing over the difference. That is why nine sections are drawn as dotted lines, one has no line drawn at all, and the boundary near Demre and the route family east of Phaselis are both marked as open questions.

Water, accommodation and transport change faster than the trail does. Our work on those is desk research rather than recent first-hand confirmation, so the guide treats them as planning context, names the source and the date it was current, and says plainly where we have nothing recent. The route analysis is ours; the underlying measurements come from the sources listed below.

Detailed sources and methodology

Sources by role

Route maintenance and current updates. Current route-maintainer information is used to check route scope, endpoints, the accommodation list and practical updates.

Section structure and variants. Published trail references and specialist hiking sources are compared to identify section boundaries, route variants, distances and ascent. Their planning taxonomies are treated as planning schemes rather than official classification.

Geometry. Route geometry is based on OpenStreetMap data (relation 51855) and published under the applicable ODbL terms. Additional GPS tracks are used only as comparison evidence for alignment, continuity and distance, and are not redistributed. Tracks that materially conflicted with the mapped route were not used. Every GPX on this page is generated from the OpenStreetMap route, with attribution carried inside the file.

Coordinates. Settlement positions currently come from a commercial mapping service and are being moved to an openly licensed source. They are kept separate from the trail waypoints used by the route geometry.

Current field conditions. Water, accommodation and transport can change quickly. Where recent first-hand confirmation is unavailable, the guide treats this information conservatively and identifies the uncertainty.

How to read our status labels

Supported by more than one source: the figure or alignment appears in at least two route representations that agree, though we have not established that they are independent of each other. Likely: the drawn line matches the source distance, reaches both endpoints and stays on the mapped route. Needs verification: sources disagree, or the line fails one of those checks; shown dotted. No current data: we hold nothing, and say so.

Dataset by dataset

Route geometry on the map
OpenStreetMap relation 51855, viewable as a public route export, accessed September 2026. Licensed under the ODbL by OpenStreetMap contributors. Lines are drawn at full mapped detail, roughly 37 m between points. Nine of the 23 stages produce a line that does not match the published stage distance; those are labelled as unverified alignment rather than corrected to fit.
Stage distances, ascent and walking times
Distances and ascent come from published stage data and are cross-checked against our own measurement of the mapped route. The published figures are the ones shown, because a shortest-path measurement understates a trail that winds. Published grades were not reused: difficulty is calculated independently from distance and ascent, and labelled as ours. Where source figures and the mapped line differ materially, the difference is left visible rather than corrected to fit.
Total length, total climbing, season
Route-maintainer information, plus a specialist trail database, an itinerary source and four full-route GPS tracks as further checks; we have not established that any of them measured the route independently of the others. Current maintainer information describes the Lycian Way as about 760 km over 35 days, covering the wider waymarked network including newer and alternative routes; three secondary sources sit near 450 km for a single end-to-end walk, and the 23 planning sections in this guide total about 447 km. These describe different scopes and are not directly comparable. Climbing is reported between 23 and 25 km of vertical gain; the published stage figures only add to about 18 km, so no total here is exact.
Accommodation
The route maintainer (Culture Routes Society) publishes an accommodation list, most recently dated February 2026; we have not contacted any business. Nothing on this page should be read as confirmation that a particular place is operating. Last checked September 2026.
Settlement coordinates
Settlement positions for 37 of the villages shown currently come from a commercial mapping service and are being moved to an openly licensed source; the remainder came with the page and sit within about a kilometre of the trail. Village markers show the settlement, not a trail junction, and are kept separate from the trail waypoints the route geometry uses.
