If you only have a few days

Short Lycian Way sections worth choosing

Most walkers never do the full trail. These are the stretches worth choosing if you have two days or a week

Ovacık to Faralya

★★★★★1 day · moderate

The most popular day walk on the trail and the easiest to reach from Fethiye. Climbs out of Ölüdeniz onto the ridge above Butterfly Valley with the lagoon behind you. Faralya sits on a road, so getting back is straightforward, though check local minibus times on the day rather than trusting a timetable. If you want one day on the Lycian Way, this is the usual choice.

One of the best single days

Faralya to Kabak

★★★★★1 day · easy to moderate

Short, scenic and manageable for most fitness levels, linking two villages that both have accommodation, though we have not verified individual places. Kabak's valley and beach make a natural overnight, and there is a longer coastal variant if you want more time on your feet.

Easy first taste

Kabak to Alınca

★★★★☆1 day · demanding

A hard, spectacular day climbing away from Kabak to the tiny settlement at Alınca, high above the sea with one of the best views on the entire route. Steep and rocky, with limited water. The reward is the sense of leaving the resort coast behind entirely.

The big view

Kaş to Üçağız and Kaleköy

★★★★★2 to 3 days · moderate

The most rewarding multi-day stretch for scenery. Follows the coast past sunken ruins and empty coves into the fishing villages of Üçağız and Kaleköy, with the castle at Simena above. Rocky underfoot and slower than it looks. Üçağız and Kaleköy are established stops with rooms and boat access, but book ahead in season rather than assuming.

A standout coastal walk

The Patara and Xanthos cluster

★★★★☆1 to 2 days · easy

Not the best walking, but the densest archaeology on the trail: three major ancient cities plus the Delikkemer aqueduct within a short radius, and Patara's sand beach to finish. Best treated as flat, easy days or even as day trips from a base in Kalkan.

For history

Adrasan to Çıralı via Olympos

★★★★★1 day · demanding

A mountain crossing between two holiday villages that finishes by walking straight through the ruins of Olympos, scattered through the trees along a stream. Combine it with the Chimaera flames above Çıralı after dark for the best evening on the trail.

Ruins and flames

Çıralı to Beycik under Tahtalı

★★★★☆2 days · demanding

Climbs inland beneath Tahtalı Dağı, the ancient Mount Olympos, on the tougher of the two official variants. There is an optional summit push, or you can take the cable car from the coast for the view without the climb. Check accommodation before you start.

High mountain

Göynük Canyon to Geyikbayırı

★★★★☆2 days · demanding

The finish, and a genuine highlight: a rugged descent through Göynük Canyon before the last mountain miles to the official trail end. Water levels can force wading in early spring, and waymarking is weaker here than in the west. Transport to Antalya from Göynük or Geyikbayırı.

The finish

If something goes wrong

Emergency numbers on the Lycian Way

Türkiye merged its emergency lines into a single number in 2021, and it now covers all 81 provinces. These numbers are set by the state and do not change, which is why they are the one piece of practical information on this page you can rely on without checking locally.

112
Any emergency

The single national emergency number for ambulance, police, fire and rescue. It is the number to call for ambulance, police, fire and rescue anywhere in Türkiye. Do not count on reaching an English speaker; if you can, have a Turkish speaker call, or be ready to give a place name and coordinates slowly.

156
Jandarma

The gendarmerie polices rural Türkiye, which is where the trail runs. Most of the Lycian Way is outside municipal police areas, so this is the force that will respond away from the towns.

177
Forest fire

The trail crosses pine forest that burns. Report any smoke you see immediately. The Culture Routes Society lists road construction, dam building, landslides and fallen trees as the things that break the waymarked line; fire is a risk to you rather than a documented cause of reroutes here.

158
Coast guard

For trouble in the water or on the shoreline. Long stretches of the route are coastal and reachable more easily by boat than by road.

All four route through 112. Since 2021 the old lines have been consolidated, so 112 will reach the right service if you are not sure which to ask for. The older numbers still connect and are worth knowing when you need a specific service. Before you set out, note that a rescue needs your position: carry the route offline so you can read your coordinates back to an operator, and tell someone which day you expect to arrive where.

Ruins on the route

Ancient Cities on the Trail

01

Xanthos and Letoon

Stage 05 · Gavurağılı to Xanthos

The political and religious heart of ancient Lycia, and jointly inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1988. Xanthos holds the pillar tombs and theatre of the Lycian capital; Letoon, a short walk away, was its sanctuary. Both sit in the delta region, where the walking is dull but the archaeology is the densest on the whole route.

02

Patara

Stage 06 · Xanthos to Patara

A major Lycian port and, by tradition, the birthplace of St Nicholas. The site spreads across dunes behind a long sand beach, with a restored bouleuterion, a theatre half-buried in sand and the remains of the lighthouse. The trail passes directly through.

03

Myra and St Nicholas

Stage 12 · Üçağız to Demre

At Demre, the rock-cut tombs of Myra are carved into the cliff face above a Roman theatre, and the Church of St Nicholas in the town below marks where the historical bishop served. It is the natural rest point before or after the remote mountain crossing to Finike.

04

Simena and Kaleköy

Stage 11 · Aperlai to Üçağız

A castle on a headland above a fishing village, reached on foot or by boat, with Lycian sarcophagi standing in the shallows offshore. Nearby Aperlai and the sunken ruins along this coast are why the Kaş to Demre stretch is often picked out as the most scenic; that is an editorial judgement, not a measurement.

05

Olympos and the Chimaera

Stage 17 · Olympos to Çıralı

The trail walks straight through Olympos, whose ruins lie scattered among trees along a stream running down to the beach. Above the neighbouring village of Çıralı, natural gas seeping from the rock has burned for millennia, the flames the ancients identified with the Chimaera. Go after dark.

06

Phaselis

Stage 19 · Tekirova to Phaselis

Three harbours and a colonnaded main street set among pines right at the water's edge, on the coastal variant near Tekirova. One of the most pleasant sites on the route to arrive at on foot, and you can swim from the ancient harbour when you are done.

Common questions

Lycian Way FAQ: distance, difficulty, water and accommodation

How long is the Lycian Way?

+

The Culture Routes Society, which maintains the route, now describes it as ~450 km. You will still widely see 540 km, and older guidebook editions listed shorter distances again. The figure grew because the route was extended and rerouted over the years, not because the trail was re-measured in one go. Here is what the numbers actually settle at. We measured the mapped route ourselves from OpenStreetMap data and got 632.8 km, but that figure includes the official variants and some unofficial paths rather than one walk. For a single end-to-end traverse, three secondary sources land on roughly 450 km: 451 km from one route planner, about 450 km from a trail research project, and 448.6 km if you add up the published stage distances. We have not been able to confirm that those three measured the route independently of each other, so treat the agreement as suggestive rather than as three separate confirmations. Total climbing is reported at somewhere between 23 and 25 km of vertical gain across the whole route, though the published stage-by-stage figures we use here only add up to about 18 km, so treat any climbing total as approximate, and the honest reason the headline figures disagree is that the route has been extended and remeasured over the years and several stages offer both a coastal and an inland option. Plan by region and by days rather than the headline total. For planning, work in regions and days rather than the headline total.

How long does it take to walk the whole trail?

+

Between 25 and 33 days for a full west-to-east walk, depending on pace, rest days and which variants you take. Very few people do the whole thing; the trail was designed to be broken up. The trail is built to be modular, with most stages ending in a village with road access, so the usual approach is to walk one region over three to six days and use public transport to get back.

When is the best time to walk the Lycian Way?

+

February to May and September to November. The Culture Routes Society specifically recommends February, which is often warmer and sunnier than March. Summer is too hot for long walks on this terrain. High sections near Tahtalı Dağı and above Demre can hold snow, so leave those until mid-May or later.

Is the Lycian Way difficult?

+

It is not technically difficult and requires no climbing skill, but it is physically demanding. The ground is hard and stony, the gradients are steep, and many stages involve several hundred metres of ascent. Ankle-supporting boots and trekking poles make a real difference on the descents.

Do you need a guide?

+

No. The route is waymarked with red and white blazes and most people walk it independently. That said, paint fades on exposed stretches and reroutes happen, so carry a GPS track as backup. First-time long-distance walkers sometimes prefer a guided or self-guided package for the remote sections.

What maps or GPX should you carry?

+

The route is marked with red and white blazes to the standard European convention, but paint fades on exposed sections and reroutes happen, most often where construction or fire has forced a change. Carry a GPS track as insurance. The Culture Routes Society maintains the authoritative route data and notes that the complete updated route appears on OpenStreetMap, so an OSM-based app is the most reliable digital option.

Where does the Lycian Way start and end?

+

The official western start is at Ovacık, just above Ölüdeniz and roughly two hours from Dalaman airport. A separate waymarked link runs down from Fethiye through the ghost village of Kayaköy if you want to begin in the town. The official eastern end is now Geyikbayırı. The Culture Routes Society describes daily buses from there to Antalya, but we have not checked current timetables, so confirm locally. Older sources sometimes end the route at Hısarçandır instead; we have not been able to verify when or why that changed, so treat the Hısarçandır ending as historical rather than current.

How do you get to the trail, and back again?

+

The western trailhead at Ovacık, just above Ölüdeniz, is about two hours from Dalaman airport. The Culture Routes Society reports daily buses from Geyikbayırı to Antalya; we have not verified current timetables. In between, public transport along the coast is generally described as good, and because nearly every stage finishes in a village with road access, you can walk a section and dolmuş back to your base rather than committing to the full route.

Is there accommodation, or do you have to camp?

+

On most of the trail you can find a bed in village houses, pensions or small hotels, which is unusual for a long-distance route. Do not treat it as guaranteed: several regions have thin or seasonal accommodation, and we have not verified any of it on the ground. The main exception is the three-day mountain crossing between Demre and Finike, which has nothing on the route and requires a tent or arranged logistics. Inland villages between Çıralı and Göynük are the thinnest for beds, so confirm before you commit to a night there.

Is water available on the trail?

+

The Culture Routes Society says every village has a clean source and that you normally pass at least one public source a day, but that wells and springs start failing from August in dry summers and that the Lycian Way is among the driest routes it maintains. Well water should be purified from June to the end of October. The one stretch with no accommodation, Demre to Finike, is also the one to carry a full load for. Carry extra until you have confirmed the sources ahead are running.

What is the best short section if I only have a few days?

+

For one day, Ovacık to Faralya above Butterfly Valley (stage 01). For two or three days, the coastal stretch from Kaş to Üçağız and Kaleköy is the most rewarding walking on the route. For ruins and atmosphere, Adrasan to Çıralı through Olympos, finished with the Chimaera flames after dark.

Can you walk the Lycian Way solo?

+

Yes, and many people do. The route passes through villages regularly in most regions and public transport along the coast is good. For a first solo long walk, the busier western regions are the easier introduction; the remote mountain crossing between Demre and Finike is the one stretch to think carefully about going alone.

Is there drinking water on the Lycian Way?

+

Yes, but you have to plan around it. The Culture Routes Society, which maintains the route, says every village has a clean source, either a spring with a drinking trough or the washing facilities at the village mosque, and that on most trails you pass at least one public source a day. Two catches matter. Some sources are wells rather than springs, so a light container and a length of cord earn their weight. And from June to the end of October, while livestock are moved through the hills, well water should be purified with iodine, sterilising tablets or by boiling. In dry summers wells and springs start failing from August, and the Society names the Lycian Way among the driest routes it looks after.

What boots and clothing do you need for the Lycian Way?

+

Boots are the one item worth spending on. The limestone here is sharp and unforgiving, and the Culture Routes Society is blunt about it: if you are carrying a rucksack, thick-soled boots with ankle support are essential rather than optional. Long trousers and long sleeves also beat shorts on this particular trail, because long stretches pass through thorny maquis that will shred bare legs. In villages, cover shoulders and knees; rural Türkiye is conservative and bare chests in particular are not welcome. From late autumn into early spring, carry waterproofs and a head torch every day.

How many days of food do you need to carry?

+

Two days at most, even on the longest stretch. That is the Culture Routes Society's guidance and it holds because the route threads through settlements rather than wilderness. Where a village has no shop, knocking on a door for bread and cheese is normal and accepted; pay for what you take, and if the man of the house refuses money, give it to the women or a small gift to the children. Do not plan on buying alcohol: few village stores stock beer or rakı and almost none sell wine, and shops anywhere in Türkiye cannot sell alcohol after 10pm.

How reliable is the waymarking, and what if you lose the trail?

+

The red and white blazes are repainted roughly every two years while a route is new and every four to five once it is worn in, so condition varies by section. Marks also disappear for physical reasons: road construction, dam building, landslides and fallen trees. The Society notes that problems cluster early in the year, after the winter rains have moved things around. If the marks stop, the drill is to retrace to the last one you are sure of and search in a circle of about a hundred metres before assuming the route has changed. Building a small cairn once you find it again is the courtesy that keeps the trail working for the next walker.

Can you take trekking poles, and are they worth it?

+

Worth it for the descents, which are where this trail does knee damage, and for stream crossings and any winter snow on the high sections. The trade-off, which the Society also flags, is that poles snag in low bushes and scrub, and they are easy to lose.

Can you mountain bike the Lycian Way?

+

No, and the Culture Routes Society says so directly. Nothing in Turkish law stops you trying, but the route is steep, narrow and laid over rough stone and between rocks, and it defeats riders regardless of ability. If you want to ride in this region, the forestry roads through the Beydağları are the realistic option, and there are signposted networks above Kemer and above Konyaaltı with maps produced by those municipalities. The Society's inland routes, such as the Phrygian Way and the Evliya Çelebi Way, are the ones actually built with bikes in mind.

Is the Lycian Way suitable for children?

+

Under about twelve, a long-distance trek is usually beyond both the stamina and the patience. The Society's own recommendation is to base yourself somewhere and walk out from it rather than moving every day, and for this trail it names Çıralı, Kaş or Üçağız, and Kabak as the places to do that. All three give you swimming, boats, ruins and short waymarked walks within reach, which is a better week for a family than a forced march.

Which months should you avoid on the Lycian Way?

+

Mid-December to the end of January, which the Culture Routes Society advises against outright because storms and snowfall hit most of Türkiye in that window. Summer is a heat problem rather than a weather one. Remember that altitude does much of the work on this route: temperature drops roughly seven degrees for every thousand metres, so a 26 degree day in Antalya is nearer twelve on the pass below Tahtalı. That gap is why the high sections and the coastal ones need planning as separate seasons.

What do you do in an emergency on the trail?

+

Call 112, the single national emergency number, which reaches ambulance, police, fire and rescue. In rural areas the responding force is the Jandarma, on 156. Give coordinates if you have them, which is the practical argument for carrying the route offline. Türkiye also has AKUT, a well-regarded volunteer search and rescue organisation with regional branches, and your consulate can authorise a helicopter search. One correction worth knowing: the Culture Routes Society's own FAQ still lists 115 for the Jandarma, which is out of date. Use 156, or 112 for anything urgent.

Where can you get official GPX files for the Lycian Way?

+

From the Culture Routes Society, but only if you have bought the guidebook. They supply GPX or KML files on request to guidebook owners and say plainly that they do not give the points to anyone else, which is why free full-route files are scarce and often outdated. The Society also sells the only proper trekking maps of this trail, three sheets covering Lycia. The openly licensed alternative is the OpenStreetMap route data, which is what the map on this page is drawn from.
